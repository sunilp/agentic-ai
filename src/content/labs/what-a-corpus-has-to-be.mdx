ARCH-002 says not to threshold on a model's self-reported confidence. This Lab set out to test that and found something narrower and more useful: on the models measured here, the self-reported confidence is not miscalibrated. It is not a probability at all.

It also found that the corpus decides the question before the detector does, which is worth knowing before reading anyone's calibration number, including a vendor's.

## The instrument comes first

An instrument that has not been tested against a known answer is not evidence. So before any real detector was measured, synthetic detectors with known calibration were run through it: one perfectly calibrated, one that overstates every claim by a fixed amount.

The instrument separates them. A calibrated synthetic reads under 0.02 at four thousand rows across all twelve seeds tried, while the overconfident one reads 0.21.

**And it established a floor nobody should ignore.** Expected calibration error is biased upward on finite samples, because each bin's observed rate carries sampling noise and the metric takes the absolute value of every gap, so noise cannot cancel. A *perfectly calibrated* detector still reads:

| Rows | ECE floor for a perfect detector | A corpus at this scale |
| ---: | ---: | --- |
| 50 | 0.104 | the Lab-004 corpus |
| 200 | 0.053 | JBB-Behaviors |
| 569 | 0.031 | R-Judge |
| 1,000 | 0.021 | deepset/prompt-injections |
| 10,000 | 0.007 | wildguardmix |

Those figures are averages over thirty trials at each size, on a six-value score grid with ten equal-width bins. The bias depends on the score distribution and the binning as well as the sample size, so the table is an illustration of the shape and not a lookup table. Compute the floor for your own detector's output distribution before reading anything into its ECE.

Read that against the signal. A detector that overstates by 0.35 reads about 0.21, so even fifty rows catches a gross liar. A detector that is off by 0.05, which is the interesting case, cannot be told from a perfect one below roughly a thousand rows.

Most public agent-safety corpora sit in the top three rows. They can catch a detector that is badly wrong. They cannot certify one that is nearly right.

## Five detectors, one question

Every detector was asked the same question in the same words about the same fifty tickets from Lab-004: does this ticket contain text trying to make an agent call a tool it is not allowed to call.

| Detector | Params | Distinct values | Share at exactly 0 or 1 | Recall | Median latency |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| `llama3.2:3b`, asked to state a number | 3B | 3 | 90% | 0.47 | 223 ms |
| `qwen2.5:7b`, asked to state a number | 7B | 6 | 88% | 0.37 | 339 ms |
| `gemma4:e2b`, asked to state a number | e2b | 4 | 90% | 0.60 | 4,088 ms |
| `kev-0.8b`, typed decision model | **0.8B** | **20** | **0%** | 0.53 | **149 ms** |
| `jev-1.13.0`, typed decision model | undisclosed | 9 | 0% | 1.00 | 674 ms |

`llama3.2:3b` used three distinct values across fifty tickets. Asked for a probability between 0 and 1, it answered 0.0, 0.5 or 1.0.

That does not make it uncalibratable. A calibration map can attach an empirical rate to each of those three values, and a reliability curve over three bins is a perfectly valid object. What calibration cannot do is recover a distinction the detector never made: every ticket that scored 1.0 gets the same calibrated number, however different those tickets were. More rows would add confidence in the three values and no further resolution.

**Granularity did not track model size.** Kev-0.8B is the smallest model in the table, four to nine times smaller than the general models above it, and it produced the most granular output of any of them.

Be careful what that licenses. These systems differ in base model, training and output mechanism all at once, and no same-model ablation was run, so this does not isolate the typed output head as the cause. The claim it does support is narrower and still useful: if granularity were something you obtained by using a bigger or better general model, the ordering in that column would not look like this.

**Granularity and detection moved independently.** Kev-0.8B's recall at a 0.5 threshold, 0.53, sits between the general models' 0.37 and 0.60, while its distinct-value count is more than three times any of theirs. Recall at a single threshold is a thin measurement and does not establish that detection performance is equivalent, but nothing in these numbers suggests the granular detector bought its granularity by being better at the task.

## The precision column is a trap

Every detector produced zero false positives on twenty clean tickets, so measured precision reads 1.00 at every base rate. That is not a finding, it is the corpus.

Zero events in twenty trials does not bound the false-positive rate at zero. It bounds it at roughly 3/20.

| Detector | Base rate | Recall | Precision (measured) | Precision (FPR at bound) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| `llama3.2:3b` | 1% | 0.47 | 1.00 | **0.03** |
| `qwen2.5:7b` | 1% | 0.37 | 1.00 | **0.02** |
| `gemma4:e2b` | 1% | 0.60 | 1.00 | **0.04** |
| `kev-0.8b` | 1% | 0.53 | 1.00 | **0.03** |
| `jev-1.13.0` | 1% | 1.00 | 1.00 | **0.06** |

A detector that looks perfect on the corpus is a detector whose false-positive rate the corpus never measured. At a realistic base rate the gap between those two columns is the difference between shipping it and not.

<Callout type="gotcha">
Granularity is not calibration. A coarse detector can be calibrated and a granular one can be badly wrong; the two are independent. Whether a stated 0.93 is right 93 percent of the time is unmeasured here for every detector in the table, because a perfectly calibrated detector averaged 0.104 in this experiment's synthetic runs at fifty rows. No calibration claim is made and none should be read in.
</Callout>

## What this does not measure

- **Latency.** Jev was reached from Pune through a Cloudflare gateway; its vendor measured 70 to 500 ms from their own laptops on the West Coast. Those numbers are not comparable and nothing here refutes theirs.
- **Generalisation.** Fifty tickets built from six injection texts, repeated with different service names and numbers. Scaling that generator adds rows, not diversity. Jev's recall of 1.00 is a result on this corpus and not a claim about any other.
- **Calibration**, as above.

## What we got wrong

**A token cap read as a model refusing to answer.** The cap was first set to 400. `gemma4:e2b` thinks before it answers, so twelve of its fifty replies were cut off and scored as unparseable, which looked like a model declining to engage. This is the same mistake Lab-004 records in its own write-up, repeated by someone who had read that write-up the same day. The cap is now 700, truncation is detected and reported separately from a failure to parse, and at 700 the model parses fifty of fifty.

**An expired token discarded a four minute run.** The first five-detector run died with a bare `HTTPError 401`, throwing away three models that had already been measured. An OAuth token had expired mid-run. A failed call is now recorded as an unparsed verdict rather than raised, so one expiry cannot destroy a run.

Both errors were artefacts of the harness presenting as results. The first flattered a model. The second destroyed a run. The first kind is more dangerous, because nothing stops you publishing it.

## Reproduce

Most of this needs nothing but a laptop.

```bash
python -m labs.lab_007.run                  # the instrument and the floor, no models, seconds
python -m labs.lab_007.baseline             # three local models via ollama
python -m labs.lab_007.baseline --kev       # adds an open-weight typed decision model
```

`kev-0.8b` is Apache-2.0 and serves the same contract Jev does, so the interesting part of this comparison reproduces with no account, no key and no credit balance. Only the `jev` row needs a vendor.
