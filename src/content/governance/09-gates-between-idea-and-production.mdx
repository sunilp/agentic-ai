Picture a release going out on a Thursday afternoon. The register says the system is approved. So do the committee minutes: a pilot, twenty named users, one team. The evaluation report is green.

On Monday someone reads the deploy log. The evaluation ran on last week's prompt. The config switched the system on for every team.

Every signature was real. None was attached to what shipped. The approvals lived in documents, and the release went through a pipeline that never read them.

In your release path today, what would have stopped that deploy?

## Gates a release can't walk around

1. **Use the framework's ten stages.** Every system, at every tier, passes the same [ten stages](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.3.0/framework/governance-operations/governance-workflow.md) from intake to retirement. Each has gate criteria and names its evidence. Your tier sets how much evidence, and who signs. Map your release steps onto the stages, and add no meeting per stage. Most gates are a check on evidence.
2. **Write down each tier's evidence pack.** The framework's [minimum evidence pack](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.3.0/framework/governance-operations/governance-workflow.md#minimum-evidence-pack-by-tier) has 17 rows, from the signed risk assessment to the signed deployment approval. Generate your checklist from the control register. Hand-kept lists drift from it, as the framework's ticketing pattern warns.
3. **Make your pipeline refuse a release without it.** Run the gate before anyone signs. Give each signer their own approval step. Let only the deploy step hold the production credential.
4. **Keep registration, approval and permission apart.** A register entry approves nothing. An approval covers one release and one scope. Runtime permission grants what the approval covers, and no more.
5. **Approve a pilot as a deployment with limits.** Write its users, data, end date, exit criteria and pause triggers into the approval, and into the credential.

**The guide's call: a gate that can't stop a release isn't a gate.** If a release can reach production with a row of its pack missing, your pack is advice. The framework says missing evidence [blocks deployment](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.3.0/framework/governance-operations/governance-workflow.md#stage-7-deployment-approval) unless an approved exception covers it, and some controls can never be excepted. Your gate lives up to that only when a missing row stops the deploy.

The call guards the way in. Leave the way out open: rolling back to the last approved release, or switching a system off, waits for no gate. That is the guide's recommendation.

You're done when a system can't deploy without its tier's evidence pack. Test it. Take a release, remove one row of its pack, and try to deploy. Your pipeline should stop and name the missing control.

**Your call.** Take your last AI release. Write down which of its approvals the pipeline read before it deployed. If the answer is none, write down where they lived.

## Ten stages and the gates between them

<ArchitectureDiagram wide src="/assets/diagrams/gov-09-ten-stages.svg" alt="The framework's ten stages with a gate before each: idea intake, risk assessment and tiering, architecture and design review, development and prompt engineering, evaluation and testing, security review and red-teaming, deployment approval, then production operations, ongoing governance, and deprecation and retirement. After stage 2, T1 and T2 take a lane with signers outside the team, and the fast lane takes T3 and T4 through the same stages with a lighter pack. Both meet the last gate before go-live. A material change means the tier is checked again at stage 2." caption="Stage 2 sets the tier, and the tier picks the lane. The fast lane runs the same gates with a lighter pack. Both lanes meet the last gate before go-live." />

Stages 1 to 7 come before production. T1 and T2 systems need sign-off from outside the team, and the [fast lane](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.3.0/framework/governance-operations/governance-workflow.md#fast-lane-t3t4) takes T3 and T4 through the same gates with less evidence and no committee. Both lanes meet the last gate, where the pack is checked and signed.

**The check.** The framework's governance gate runs in your pipeline. It works out which controls apply to the system's tier and type, and fails the job when one has no evidence, an evaluation misses its threshold, or an exception is invalid. Its result file is your evidence that the deployment gates passed (DA-003). The framework states its limit [plainly](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.3.0/reference-implementations/README.md#what-the-gate-does-not-do): "It checks presence, not adequacy." People judge whether the evidence is any good.

**The signatures.** At T1, the AI Risk Committee [signs the deployment](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.3.0/framework/operating-model/roles-responsibilities.md#approvals-by-tier), with the CISO, and the DPO when personal data is processed. At T2, the Head of AI and the Business Unit Head. At T3, the team lead and the business owner. At T4, the team lead. Run the gate first, so your signers read a pack the pipeline has already found complete.

[DORA](https://dora.dev/capabilities/streamlining-change-approval/) found that approval by people outside the team, such as a change advisory board, has "a negative impact on software delivery performance", and found no evidence that it went with lower change fail rates. It recommends peer review "supplemented by automation". The framework still puts outside signers on T1 and T2 deployments. Use them to judge risk, which no pipeline can do, and let the pipeline do the checking.

**No way round.** A gate stops nothing while someone else holds the production credential. The framework's [GitHub pattern](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.3.0/reference-implementations/README.md#deployment-approval-environments-with-required-reviewers-da-002) gives each signing role its own environment, with self-review prevented, and the deploy job waits for all of them. Restrict each environment to release tags. Now only the deploy job holds the credential, and it starts after a passing gate and every signature. Then check who can edit those settings. An admin who can deploy by hand has a route around every gate you build. The guide's recommendation: alert on any production deploy with no gate result.

**Evidence tied to the release.** This is where the opening failed. The gate records a hash of every evidence file and of the evaluation report. The framework's [promotion rule](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.3.0/reference-implementations/model-registry.md#promotion-rule) moves the production label only to a version with a passing gate result, an approval reference, a tier that matches the register and no expired exception. Generate the evaluation report in the same pipeline run, on the release's own prompts, and last week's report can't approve this week's prompt.

**Three records.** Your register entry says the system exists and where it stands: proposed, in development, pilot or production. It approves nothing. Your approval is the signed decision for one release and one scope. Your permission is what the system can reach at runtime: its credential, users, data and, for an agent, tools. When the permission reaches further than the approval, the system is running outside it.

**Pilots.** The framework [registers](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.3.0/framework/governance-operations/ai-inventory.md#what-goes-in-the-inventory) pilots that touch real data, and a pilot entry needs a tier, a deployed date and a next review date. The framework has no separate pilot gate. In its worked example of a pilot, an [agentic research assistant](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.3.0/examples/agentic-research-assistant/risk-assessment.md#conditions-of-approval-ai-risk-committee-2026-01-25), the committee applied every T1 control and approved it with conditions: five named analysts for three months, no expansion without a new risk assessment, and a pause if the hallucination rate broke the T1 cap.

The guide's recommendation follows from that example. Before the pilot, you work on test data with no production credential (Step 3). The pilot passes the deployment gate at its tier, with the full pack, signed by the people who would sign production. It has real people and real data, and the pack is the minimum for both. Its approval carries the limits: named users, named data, an end date, exit criteria and pause triggers. The framework's GenAI [deployment criteria](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.3.0/framework/llm-lifecycle/deployment-gates.md#5-operational-readiness-and-approval-stages-7-and-8) already ask for a deployment plan, canary, shadow or direct, with rollback criteria. A pilot is that plan with names in it, staged the way the [guardrail lifecycle](/architecture/006-the-guardrail-lifecycle/) stages a detector.

Put the limits in the credential too, expiring on the end date. A pilot that reaches its date with no production decision stops. That is the [approval gate](/patterns/approval-gate/) rule for a clock that runs out: expire, never proceed. Production is a new approval. Wider use is a change of scope, which the framework counts as material. The re-tiering check runs, the pilot's evidence joins the pack, the gate runs again and your tier's signers sign the wider scope.

A pilot that can't meet a control needs an [exception](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.3.0/framework/governance-operations/governance-workflow.md#exceptions-and-risk-acceptance), like any release. The research assistant went live under one, with every output reviewed in full until a re-run passed. The word pilot waives nothing.

<RunningExample>
The support assistant is T1, so its pack has all 17 rows at T1 depth. Most of the work sits in a few: a full DPIA, because customer emails are personal data; the architecture review board's sign-off; full validation by Model Risk Management; a red team of at least 200 cases, including manual testing; staffed human oversight; and a tested rollback, which here means switching drafts off so agents write by hand. The gate runs first. The AI Risk Committee then signs the deployment, and the CISO and the DPO co-sign.

Step 5's shortcut put ten agents on live tickets and called it a pilot. Here is the governed version, with conditions in the approval. The details are made up.

```yaml
system_id: "support-reply-assistant-v0.1"
deployment_status: "pilot"
approved_by: ["AI Risk Committee", "CISO", "DPO"]
conditions:
  users: "10 named support agents, trained before their first review"
  data: "Live customer emails on the billing queue only"
  ends: "2027-03-31"
  pause_if:
    - "Any S1 or S2 incident"
    - "Online scoring breaks a T1 threshold"
  exit:
    - "Production evaluation meets T1 thresholds for the whole pilot"
    - "Override rate reviewed, and investigated if below 2 percent"
    - "Every near-miss report closed as a test or a control change"
```

The credential follows the conditions: those ten agents, that queue, until the end date. To go wider, the team updates the risk assessment for the new scope, adds the pilot's evidence to the pack and runs the gate again. The same three sign the production deployment, and the entry moves from `pilot` to `production`.
</RunningExample>

## Who holds the gate at each size

The stages and the packs stay the same at any size, and who holds each gate changes.

<Track for="small">
Your team of three to five can hold most gates of a T3 or T4 system. Put the framework's gate in CI, pinned to one commit. Give the team lead an approval step, and at T3 the business owner too. They must be [two different people](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.3.0/framework/governance-operations/governance-workflow.md#small-teams). Three things stay outside: AI Risk's vendor assessment when a T3 system calls an external API, the DPO's screening when you process personal data, and the T3 red team, which comes from Security or another team. Ask for all three at intake.

You can't hold a T1 or T2 gate alone. Validation, the risk approval and the deployment sign-off come from outside, so book them at intake too. A T3 pilot needs the same two signatures as production.
</Track>

<Track for="enterprise">
A platform team runs the gate as shared code, called from your repository template (Step 6). The guide's recommendation is that AI Risk owns what the gate reads: the controls, the thresholds and the approver roles. The framework's [ticketing pattern](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.3.0/reference-implementations/ticketing-workflow.md#transition-rules) gives each stage a state, refuses to let a release skip one, and never lets the requester approve.

Your committee does not click buttons. In the framework's pattern, the chair or secretary approves after the decision and records the minute reference. The committee [meets monthly](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.3.0/framework/operating-model/review-cadence.md#monthly-ai-risk-committee), and convenes out of cycle within 10 business days when a decision can't wait. Book your pilot and production decisions at intake. Track open exceptions by tier. The framework reads a rising count as a policy too rigid, or risk acceptance becoming a habit. Step 20 builds the platform this runs on.
</Track>

<Shortcut quote="The pilot went fine. Turn it on for everyone.">
**Why it's tempting.** Your users like it. The gate passed once, the committee signed once, and the next slot is weeks away.

**Why it bites.** The approval covered ten agents on one queue. Each new queue brings new emails, new policies to get wrong, and agents nobody trained. The pack described the pilot. Now the system runs outside its approval, and the register still says pilot.

**The forcing function.** Put the limits in the credential: named users, one queue, an end date. Going wider needs a new credential, and your platform issues one only against an approval that covers the wider scope. On the paths you control, quiet widening is impossible by construction. Scoped credentials are the guide's recommendation.
</Shortcut>

<DeepDive>

### The ten stages

The stages are idea intake; risk assessment and tiering; architecture and design review; development and prompt engineering; evaluation and testing; security review and red-teaming; deployment approval; production operations; ongoing governance; and deprecation and retirement. Each sets out its gate criteria, its evidence by control ID, a table by tier and who decides.

Two orderings matter most. The first is no build before approval. The control register marks the risk assessment, its approval and the design controls "before development". Your pipeline gate can't enforce that, because it doesn't check dates. A ticket workflow can, by opening the build state only after design review closes. The second is monitoring before traffic. At Stage 8, AI Operations confirms monitoring is live before any traffic flows.

Gates go on after launch. A failed revalidation sends a system back through Stage 5, and nothing is decommissioned until its evidence is archived.

Keep one gate model. The framework's [deployment gates](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.3.0/framework/llm-lifecycle/deployment-gates.md#overview) call themselves "not a separate gate model". They are the detailed checks at the workflow's stages. If you already run a change process, map it onto the ten stages. A second process beside it leaves teams to pick the easier one.

### The fast lane

T3 and T4 systems take the same ten stages with less evidence and no committee. The team lead signs off the risk assessment, picks the model from the approved vendor list and signs the deployment. At T3 the business owner co-signs. The pack drops the impact assessment, architecture review, guardrail evidence, incident playbook, rollback plan and human oversight. The non-waivable list stays: audit logging, the prohibited-use check, zero unresolved Critical red-team findings, and a DPIA where the law requires one.

The guide's call: the fast lane is a lighter pack on the same path. Run the same gate code, and let it read the tier.

A system leaves the lane when a re-tiering check gives T2 or above. The check runs at every formal review and on any material change. Common triggers: it starts serving customers, sends customer personal data to an external model, gains any tool use, or influences a regulated decision. A proposed change that raises the tier goes back through Stage 2, and the changed version needs the new tier's pack before it goes live. If the change has already happened, the missing controls run under exceptions until the evidence exists.

### Retroactive governance

You start with systems already live, and a gate can't stop a release that already happened. The framework's [route](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.3.0/framework/governance-operations/governance-workflow.md#retroactive-governance) starts with a list. Tier every production system, with the override checks. Walk the control register for each one and mark every control as evidence exists, evidence missing or not applicable. Fix T1 gaps first, then T2, T3 and T4. For each gap, name who produces the evidence, by when, and what reduces the risk meanwhile. The deadlines are three months for T1, six for T2 and twelve for T3 and T4, "non-negotiable for regulated environments".

The guide's recommendation: put the gate in front of each live system's next release, whatever its gaps. Its gap matrix becomes its first `governance.yaml`. Link the evidence that exists. Enter each gap still open at that release in the exception register, with compensating controls and an expiry. From then on, its releases pass or fail like anyone else's.

</DeepDive>

You now have gates your pipeline enforces: ten stages, a pack per tier, signers who read a complete pack, and pilots that end unless someone signs them into production. The gate still trusts what it reads. It checks the numbers an evaluation report declares, and it can't tell whether the cases resemble real use or the red team was independent. Step 10 makes evidence block releases: suites sized to mean something, pass marks judged on confidence bounds, and red teams kept apart from the people who built the system.
