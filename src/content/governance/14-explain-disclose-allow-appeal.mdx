Picture a customer disputing a late fee. She writes to ask why she was charged. The reply says the fee was applied "in line with our policies, using automated tools that keep our decisions consistent".

She writes again. Which policy? Did anyone look at her account? Can someone take a second look? The next reply repeats the first, word for word, signed "The Team".

Everything in it is true. She has been told a machine was involved. She has had an explanation, of a kind. Nothing she does from here will change the fee.

This step is about the person on the receiving end. When your system gets someone's case wrong, what can they find out, and who can they reach that is able to put it right?

## Three promises to the person affected

Disclosure, explanation and appeal sound like one promise. They are three, and each fails on its own.

- **Disclosure.** The person knows AI was involved in what they received.
- **Explanation.** They can find out why, in terms that fit them.
- **Recourse.** They can contest it, and reach a person who can change the outcome.

The customer above got the first promise and none of the third.

1. **Find who is on the receiving end.** Write down who gets each system's output, and who is decided about. The framework's [impact assessment](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/templates/impact-assessment-template.md#22-recourse-mechanisms) asks the question plainly: can a customer challenge or appeal a decision made by this system?
2. **Disclose where the person meets the output.** The framework's [rule](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/framework/responsible-ai/transparency-standards.md#user-interaction-disclosure) covers any system where someone interacts with AI, directly or indirectly. Customer communications carry a [visible label](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/framework/responsible-ai/transparency-standards.md#labeling-by-content-destination) and [metadata](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/framework/responsible-ai/transparency-standards.md#ai-generated-content-metadata). A decision about people can also owe a legal notice, under control DE-009.
3. **Explain from the record.** Tell the person which facts and which rule produced the outcome, and who checked it, using only what your logs can prove. At T1 and T2 the audit trail must [rebuild](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/framework/compliance/prompt-audit-trail.md#decision-reconstruction) any output from its request ID. At T1 the framework also asks for full source attribution and a [human-readable explanation](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/framework/responsible-ai/transparency-standards.md#output-explainability-requirements) of how the output was derived.
4. **Send every contest to someone who can change the outcome.** The guide recommends a person who holds the authority, can see the record and didn't produce what is contested. A contest never goes back to the system.
5. **Log each request and what came of it.** Control [RV-005](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/framework/governance-operations/control-register.md#stage-9-ongoing-governance) requires a log for systems that make or support decisions with legal or similarly significant effects on people: the request, its deadline, the explanation sent, and whether the review upheld or changed the outcome, with the reviewer's name. Where no law sets one, the framework's default is 30 days. The guide recommends that log for every system whose output leaves your organisation.
6. **Treat a changed outcome as a finding.** The framework lets you [declare an incident](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/framework/operating-model/escalation-paths.md#alert-priority-to-incident-severity) straight from a customer complaint. The guide adds a search: find every case like it, and turn the fault into an evaluation case.

**The guide's call: if a person cannot contest it, you have not explained it.** People ask why so they can push back. An explanation with no route to someone who can act leaves the outcome where it was. A route with no explanation fails too. Nobody can contest what they can't see.

This step is done when an affected person can find out, and contest the outcome. The guide's test is from outside. Have someone who didn't build the system contest a planted wrong answer through the real channel. Follow it until a person with authority records an outcome.

**Your call.** Take the AI system whose output reaches the most people outside your organisation. Write down what someone who thinks it got their case wrong can find out today. Then write down who can change the outcome, and how they would hear about it.

## The route a contest travels

<ArchitectureDiagram wide src="/assets/diagrams/gov-14-contest-route.svg" alt="The route a contest travels, left to right: the person affected, first contact, the reviewer and the decider, then back to the person with the outcome and its reasons, in writing. First contact, who logs it and starts the clock: the case, the request as written, the deadline. The decider, who can change the outcome: the reviewer's finding and every case like this one. Underneath, the auditor reads the log afterwards: every request, its deadline, whether the outcome was upheld or changed, and who decided. A contest ends with a person who can change the outcome. Each stop sees something different. Design each view on purpose.

**The person affected** sees the output and its label. Give her the rule that applied, when it took effect, the facts about her that mattered and who sent the reply. Model internals answer a question she didn't ask.

**First contact** is whoever opens her reply. Log it, give her a reference and start the clock. Don't make her name her request correctly. "You told me 30 days" is a contest, so file it as one.

**The reviewer** didn't produce the outcome. They work from the rebuilt case: the input, the sources retrieved, the prompt and model versions, the output, and what was sent.

**The decider** can change the outcome. For a support reply, that is someone who can change what the customer is owed. The decision goes back to the person in writing, with reasons. It goes into your log too. The auditor reads that log afterwards, across every case.

**A generated rationale is a claim to check.** Ask a model why it wrote something and it will tell you, fluently. That account is more output, made the same way, and no trace of how the first answer was made. [Turpin and colleagues](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/ed3fea9033a80fea1376299fa7863f4a-Abstract-Conference.html) biased models toward wrong answers, for instance by reordering the answer options in the worked examples so the correct one was always "(A)". The models frequently wrote explanations that rationalised the biased answers, and systematically left the bias out. In a [2025 study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.05410), two reasoning models mentioned a planted hint they had used in 25% and 39% of cases, on average. Give your reviewer the rationale as a lead. Then check it against the record. Never send it to a person as the reason. The framework asks T1 systems for a reasoning trace. The guide reads that as the logged record, never the model's account of itself.

**What the law asks.** Disclosure attaches to the system: Article 50 of the AI Act has applied since 2 August 2026, whatever the risk class. Recourse attaches to decisions about people, under Article 22 of the GDPR and Article 86 of the AI Act. The support assistant decides nothing about anyone. Neither article reaches it, and nor does RV-005. The guide recommends the route anyway: a customer who got a wrong answer can find out, and contest it.

<RunningExample>
The assistant drafts, and a person sends. The details here are illustrative.

Back to Step 11. Four customers were told the refund window was 30 days, then got a corrected reply saying 14. One writes back: "You told me 30 days." She had planned around it.

**What she can find out.** The answer names the policy, a 14-day window, and the date it took effect. It says the first reply was drafted with AI and sent by a named support agent, and that the 30 days was the company's error, from old wording in the drafting tool.

**How she contests.** She replies to the corrected email. The agent who opens it logs a contest, gives her a reference and passes it to a team lead who sent none of the four replies. The assistant drafts nothing in a contested thread, as the guide recommends.

**What the reviewer sees.** The audit trail rebuilds the first reply: the draft, the reply as sent with no edits, the prompt and model versions, and the refund page retrieval returned, which said 14. Asked on a replay why it wrote 30, the model gives a tidy account about plan terms. The record shows what Step 11 found: an example in the system prompt that still said 30.

**Who decides.** What a customer is owed is the business owner's call. The Head of Customer Support decides for all four customers, including the three who never wrote back: each keeps the 30 days the company promised. She records the decision. The log entry closes.

**What the label says.** The framework puts a visible label on any AI-drafted communication sent to a customer. Each sent reply ends: "Drafted with AI assistance, checked and sent by [first name]. If anything looks wrong, reply and a person will review it." Its record carries the framework's metadata.

That label is the framework's rule. The company has customers in the EU, and EU law doesn't require it: the Commission's guidelines put replies that staff properly review and send outside Article 50(1). The machine-readable mark is the Act's rule. The company is the assistant's provider, and the drafts are generated text. Whether the mark must survive an agent's edits is open, so the team carries it onto every sent reply and Compliance writes the question down.
</RunningExample>

## Who answers a contest at your size

The route is the same at every size. The people standing on it change.

<Track for="small">
Three roles cover it. Whoever reads the shared inbox logs the contest. Someone who didn't do the original work reviews it. Your team lead decides. Keep the log in your ticket tool with RV-005's columns. When nobody uninvolved is free, the guide's recommendation is that the team lead reviews and decides, and the log says so.

Disclosure doesn't shrink with the tier. DE-006 and DE-007 apply from T1 to T4, so a T4 tool that writes to customers carries the same label and mark. Explanation does shrink: source attribution where it applies at T3, best effort at T4.
</Track>

<Track for="enterprise">
Route AI contests into the complaints process you already run. The guide adds one field, the system ID from the register, so every contest finds its system and its owner. The system owner owns the log, and each formal review reads it. A missed statutory deadline goes to Compliance, and RV-005 records it as a compliance breach.

Read contest counts with care. A low count fits two stories: people are content, or they can't find the route. Only a test from outside tells them apart, so set no target for the count.
</Track>

<Shortcut quote="Put 'drafted with AI' in the footer and we're done.">
**Why it's tempting.** It is one line. It ships today. It even looks like the transparency standard's own example.

**Why it bites.** A label says what made the reply. It says nothing about why, or what to do when the reply is wrong. A customer who spots a wrong fee under "drafted with AI assistance" knows whom to blame and has nowhere to go.

**The forcing function.** Control [DE-007](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/framework/governance-operations/control-register.md#stage-4-development--prompt-engineering) blocks deployment until the label rules by destination and the marking design are evidenced, with detection tests on sample outputs. The guide adds two lines to that evidence: the label names the route, and a planted contest has reached the decider through it before go-live.
</Shortcut>

<DeepDive>

### Explanations by audience

NIST's [four principles](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.IR.8312) of explainable AI hold two tests apart. An explanation is meaningful when its intended consumer understands it, and accurate when it correctly reflects the reason for the output. Fluent text can pass the first and fail the second. NIST adds that one-size-fits-all explanations don't exist, so write one per reader.

- **The person affected:** the facts that mattered, the rule, the outcome and the route.
- **The reviewer:** the case rebuilt, with what the person in the loop saw and changed.
- **The auditor:** the system across cases, from the model card, the evaluations and the request log.

The framework's [model card template](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/templates/model-card-template.yaml#L78-L85) records who can see individual explanations. Its credit scoring example [fills it in](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/examples/credit-scoring-model/model-card.yaml#L118-L120): underwriters, compliance, and customers through a simplified version in adverse action notices. Fill it in for every T1 system you run. The card describes your system. It never tells one person why their outcome happened.

### Disclosure and marking

[Article 50(1)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj) makes providers tell people when they interact directly with an AI system, unless that is obvious. The Commission's [guidelines](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/guidelines-transparency-obligations-providers-and-deployers-ai-systems) exclude customer service staff who use an AI tool to write to customers, where people properly review and send the replies as the customer's main contact. The mere chance of review doesn't count, so the exclusion rests on your review being real.

Article 50(2) has no such route. A provider of a generative system marks its outputs in a machine-readable format, detectable as AI-generated. The relevant exceptions cover standard editing, such as grammar fixes, and outputs that don't substantially alter the input. A drafted reply is neither. Mixing in human writing doesn't take your output out of scope. Build on a vendor model and run the system under your own name, and you are its provider. The [Digital Omnibus](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj) gave generative systems placed on the market before 2 August 2026 until 2 December 2026 to add the marking.

The framework's [metadata fields](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/framework/responsible-ai/transparency-standards.md#ai-generated-content-metadata) carry the mark, among them `ai_generated`, `human_reviewed` and `source_attribution`.

### Recourse

Under [Article 22 of the GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj), a person has the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing that has legal or similarly significant effects on them. Where a contract or the person's explicit consent allows one, the controller gives at least human intervention, a chance to state a view and the right to contest. The answer to a request under Articles 15 to 22 is due within one month of receipt, extendable by two further months where necessary (Article 12(3)).

[Article 86](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj) of the AI Act covers decisions a deployer takes on the output of an Annex III high-risk system, apart from the critical infrastructure systems in point 2, with legal or similarly significant effects the person considers adverse to their health, safety or fundamental rights. The person can obtain a clear and meaningful explanation of the system's role and the decision's main elements, unless Union law already gives that right. After the Omnibus, the Annex III rules apply from 2 December 2027.

The framework's [credit scoring example](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/examples/credit-scoring-model/risk-assessment.md#system-overview) approves within policy automatically, an underwriter decides every proposed decline, and any applicant can ask for human review of an automated decision. Credit scoring is Annex III point 5(b), so once those rules apply, an applicant in the EU declined on the score can ask for an Article 86 explanation. The underwriter is the person your route needs, if they truly decide. One who signs whatever the score says is a formality.

The framework's own [NIST mapping](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/framework/compliance/nist-ai-rmf-mapping.md#gaps-and-recommendations) lists an appeal process for people affected by AI outcomes as a gap. RV-005 covers decisions with legal or similar effects, and your log covers the rest.

</DeepDive>

You now have three promises, kept apart: a label where people meet the output, explanations built from the record, and a logged route to a person who can change the outcome. Each one leans on a person. The label says someone checked the reply. Step 11's four wrong replies each went out through a person who sent them unedited. Step 15 makes human oversight real.
