Two requests reach your intake on the same morning. The IT team wants an assistant that answers staff questions from the internal wiki. The lending team wants a model that scores every loan application.

One process for both fails one of them. Hold the wiki assistant to the credit model's bar, with a committee sign-off, full independent validation and a challenger model, and the IT team learns to go around you. Hold the credit model to the wiki assistant's bar, a peer review and a monthly look at its metrics, and it ships without independent validation.

So one of them waits. Which one, and what does it wait for? Write your answer down before you read on.

## The move

Before any score, ask whether this should be AI at all. The framework's [use case assessment](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.0.0/templates/genai-use-case-assessment.md#0-should-this-use-genai-at-all) opens with eight questions from its [when to say no](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.0.0/framework/governance-operations/when-to-say-no.md) standard. Would the model generate a fee or a balance instead of taking it from a system of record? Would an agent take a write action nobody can undo, with no human confirming it? Would plain search or a rules engine do the job? A yes means stop or redesign. Saying no at intake, the standard notes, "costs a conversation".

The [Step 0 screen](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.0.0/framework/risk-classification/genai-risk-matrix.md#step-0-prohibited-use-screen) comes first. Check the use against the prohibited practices in [Article 5 of the EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj), such as social scoring, and against your own list from Step 2. A match is rejected. It gets no tier and no exception. A redesign starts again at intake, as a new use case.

Everything that passes gets a tier, by one rule:

1. **Score five dimensions** from the GenAI or the traditional ML matrix, each as Critical, High, Medium or Low.
2. **The highest dimension sets the tier.** Any Critical gives T1. Otherwise any High gives T2, any Medium gives T3, and all Low gives T4.
3. **Check every override.** Nine facts about a system set a floor of T1 or T2. Overrides can raise the tier, never lower it.
4. **The approver may raise it** with a recorded rationale. Nobody can lower it below what the scoring and overrides give.

**The guide's call: govern by tier, not by project, and decide the no-list before the first request.** Every system at a tier meets the same approver, the same evidence and the same reviews, so no team negotiates its own process. A no written in advance is a rule. A no improvised in the room is a fight with whoever loves the demo.

## How it works

<ArchitectureDiagram wide src="/assets/diagrams/gov-04-tiering.svg" alt="Tiering: a prohibited-use screen, five scored dimensions, the highest wins, overrides only raise, the approver may raise but never lower." />

A GenAI system, agents included, is scored on hallucination severity, data exposure, prompt injection susceptibility, output controllability and third-party model dependency. A traditional model is scored on decision autonomy, data sensitivity, regulatory exposure, reversibility and scale of impact. A system with both kinds of model is scored on both, and the higher tier applies.

The highest score wins because one severe exposure is enough to hurt someone. A sum would let four quiet dimensions outvote a loud one.

Overrides are yes-or-no questions. Does it directly influence a credit decision? Does it send content to customers with no human review of each output? Is it an agent, even a read-only one? Each yes sets a floor.

## How it scales

The rule holds at any size. What changes is who prepares the assessment and who signs it.

<Track for="small">
Have your tech lead score each system. In the framework the product owner prepares the assessment, a role the team lead can hold in a small team.

Your T3 and T4 systems take the framework's [fast lane](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.0.0/framework/governance-operations/governance-workflow.md#fast-lane-t3t4): the same stages, less evidence, no committee, and the team lead's sign-off. Any override puts a system at T2 or above, outside the fast lane.

A [small team](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.0.0/START-HERE.md#small-teams) can't govern a T1 or T2 system on its own. Approval and validation come from outside it: in the framework, AI Risk and a second-line validator at T2, the AI Risk Committee and Model Risk Management at T1. If your company has no second line, name who plays that part before your first T2 system arrives, and book them at intake.
</Track>

<Track for="enterprise">
The product owner prepares the assessment. The tier picks the approver: the AI Risk Committee for T1, AI Risk in the second line for T2, a team lead for T3, and for T4 a team lead who acknowledges the self-assessment. Model Risk Management validates every T1 tier independently, as the framework's [decision process](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.0.0/framework/risk-classification/risk-matrix.md#decision-process) requires.
</Track>

<Shortcut quote="Call it T3 so we can ship Friday.">
**Why it's tempting.** A lower tier means fewer gates. A team lead signs instead of a committee, and a peer review replaces independent validation.

**Why it bites.** The tier sets every later control. Call a T1 system T3, and its validation, monitoring and reviews are all sized for a different system. Each later gate then checks it against the wrong bar.

**The forcing function.** Record the five scores, each with its justification, and the override answers in the register entry, and compute the tier from them, so nobody can type one in. Overrides and approvers can only raise it, and nobody can lower it below what the scores and overrides give. Computing the tier is the guide's recommendation. The one-way rule is the framework's.
</Shortcut>

### The override list

The [canonical list](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.0.0/framework/risk-classification/genai-risk-matrix.md#tier-override-rules) applies in every matrix: GenAI, traditional ML, multimodal and agentic.

The four safeguards in items 3 and 8 are zero data retention by the vendor, a regional endpoint that meets data residency requirements, a signed DPA (data processing agreement), and PII redaction or minimisation before the call. Miss one and the system is automatic T1. The when-to-say-no standard goes further. Fix the safeguard before the design review passes. T1 governance can't repair a data flow you can't evidence.

### Why every agent is at least T2

An agent with read-only tools still acts on content it can't trust. A retrieved document can carry instructions, and an early error in a multi-step plan carries through every later step. So item 6 sets T2 for any agent, read-only included, and for any multi-agent system.

Write access to production, or authority to move money, contact customers or change access, makes it T1 under item 4. The [agentic risk standard](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.0.0/framework/risk-classification/agentic-ai-risk.md#mandatory-risk-tier-classification) adds one more route: an agent that processes PII, MNPI or regulated data scores Critical on data exposure, so it is T1 even with read-only tools. Step 18 sets what an agent can touch.

### Re-tiering

The framework re-runs the check at every formal governance review and on any material change to scope, data, autonomy or regulation. A model change, a provider update or a prompt change counts too. The common ways out of the fast lane: the system starts serving customers, gains a tool, sends customer personal data to an external model API, or starts to influence a regulated decision.

A change that raises the tier goes back through assessment, and the changed version needs the new tier's evidence before it goes live. If the change has already happened, each missing control needs an approved, time-limited exception until the evidence exists. Non-waivable controls get none.

### When to say no

Two of the standard's eight positions are the Step 0 screen and the simpler-method test. The other six are design rules. Take figures with legal effect from a system of record. Keep a human on adverse regulated decisions. Allow no autonomous write that can't be undone or capped. Send no customer or regulated data to an external API without evidenced residency and zero retention. Launch nothing whose evaluation can't show its threshold is met. Run no business-critical process on a model you can't replace.

A team that thinks a position shouldn't apply takes its case to the tier approver, who decides and records it. Two can't be set aside at all: the prohibited-use screen, and the kill switch in the no-undo position, because both rest on non-waivable controls.

Your no-list also grows with the law. From 2 December 2026, the [Digital Omnibus on AI](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj) adds two practices to Article 5: AI systems that generate intimate imagery of an identifiable person without their consent, and systems that generate child sexual abuse material. Check the safeguards of every image and video tool you run.

### The aggregate score

Record the sum of the five scores, from 5 to 20, for context only. It never sets the tier. The knowledge search sums to 8. One Critical dimension and four Low ones also sum to 8, and that system is T1.

</DeepDive>
