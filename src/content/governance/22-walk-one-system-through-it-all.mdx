The audit committee has seen the programme slides: a one-page policy, a full register, tiers, gates and a dashboard that is mostly green. Then one member asks for something smaller. Show us one system. The riskiest one, from the idea to the reply it sent this morning.

The governance lead opens the register entry. The risk assessment link works. The evaluation folder holds three reports, and nobody noted which one shipped. The red-team report is in someone's email. The pilot had an end date. Nobody can find the decision that took it into production.

Most of what the slides promised did happen. Whether it happened to this system, at this version, only the records can say.

Can you walk your highest-risk system through every step of this guide and open the record each one left?

## Pull the record each step left behind

1. **Pick your highest-risk system.** Take the highest-tier system that real people use. If two tie, take the one you know least about. Never the tidiest.
2. **Hand the walk to someone who didn't build it.** They ask. The system owner opens each record. At T1 the framework has the seat ready: its [review cadence](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/framework/operating-model/review-cadence.md#annual-activities) gives MRM and Compliance a mock examination of every T1 system each year.
3. **Bring records, never summaries.** The owner brings the register entry. The walker brings the 22 steps and the controls the register applies to this tier and type. Every record is opened where it was made, with its own date, version and author.
4. **Go in the order things happened.** At each step, ask for the record and check three things. Does it exist? Does it name a version that ran? Do the people it names still hold their roles?
5. **Write every gap down, and say which kind.** Some steps were never done. Others were done and never recorded. Both go on the list with an owner and a date. The second kind is worse. Anyone asking for proof counts it as not done. The team thinks it is covered, so nobody plans a fix. And it shows that record keeping broke somewhere, which casts doubt on every other record.
6. **End at the permission.** Set what the system can reach today (its credential, users, data and tools) against what its last approval covers. Step 9 kept those records apart for this moment. Any reach beyond the approval is the first finding.

**The guide's call: governance is proven on one real system, not on a slide.** A slide shows the machinery. It can't show that the machinery ran on the system that matters, at the version running today. Only that system's records can, opened by someone who didn't write them.

This step is done when you can trace one high-risk system through every step and show the evidence at each. The guide counts a written gap with an owner as traced. A step nobody asked about isn't.

**Your call.** Name your highest-risk system. Before you open anything, list the steps where you expect to find no record. Then walk, and compare.

The figure groups the trail by part, so you can find any step. Each record has a home: the register, the pipeline, the model card, the audit store or the committee's minutes. The walker opens it there.

Eight records are in brick, more than a third of the trail. Each shows something failing or refused, from a free cancellation a support agent caught to the morning the gateway went down. A programme slide leaves all eight out. Yet they are the strongest evidence here. Each shows a control acting when it had to. If your own trail holds no failure at all, ask whether anyone tested the system hard, or whether someone tidied its records.

The walk follows events, and they ran out of step order. The work of Steps 12 and 13, and the signing of Step 16's limits, came before the first release candidate. Steps 7, 11, 14, 15 and 17 happened in the pilot. The move to production, which no earlier step tells, sits between 17 and 18.

## A fast-lane trail and a first audit

The walk is the same at any size. The length of the trail and the walker change.

<Track for="small">
If your riskiest system is T3, it runs the framework's [fast lane](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/framework/governance-operations/governance-workflow.md#fast-lane-t3t4), and its trail is shorter. Its pack holds at most 12 of the 17 rows. Steps 13, 15 and 18 require nothing at T3. Bias testing and human oversight are T1 and T2 controls, and an agent with a single tool is T2 at least. Give each such step one line saying why, as the case file does for the controls it doesn't need.

The rest lives in your repository: the register file, `governance.yaml`, the CI report, the gate result, two signatures, and the scan and 50 manual cases another team ran. Ask someone from that team to walk it at your yearly review.
</Track>

<Track for="enterprise">
Walk your first T1 system before an auditor picks one. The framework wants an [examination pack](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/framework/compliance/audit-trail-requirements.md#regulatory-examination-readiness) you can assemble within 48 hours, a named point person, and a yearly mock examination of every T1 system. Make this walk that examination.

If the system predates your programme, the walk is the framework's [retroactive route](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/framework/governance-operations/governance-workflow.md#retroactive-governance). Mark every control as evidence exists, evidence missing or not applicable. Each missing item gets an owner, a date and an interim control. T1 gaps close within three months.

Expect a long list. The assistant's trail is nearly complete because it was governed from intake, and because it is made up. The guide's recommendation: choose each later walk's system at random from your T1 list. Then nobody can tidy one system for the auditor.
</Track>

<Shortcut quote="Turn it into a case study deck for the audit committee.">
**Why it's tempting.** A deck takes an afternoon. The committee wants a story. You pick the parts.

**Why it bites.** A deck summarises records, so it proves nothing. The people under review write it, today, about the version they remember. The failed release candidate drops out. So does the gap. And once one missing record is written up to fill a slide, an auditor must doubt every other record in the file.

**The forcing function.** The walk's record is a list of links, each to a record where it was made, with its own date, version and author. Nothing written for the walk counts. A record written to fill a gap carries the day it was written, and the gap stays open until the control runs again. This is the guide's recommendation.
</Shortcut>

### A bank's chatbot on the same trail

The framework's T1 worked example is a [governance case file](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/examples/customer-service-chatbot/governance-case-file.md) for a different system, `cs-assistant-retail-v1.2`. It is a customer service assistant in a bank's mobile app and online banking chat, and it answers customers itself. Nobody reads each answer first. So it runs under Pattern 3 with all five safeguards, and override trigger 2 makes it T1. The support assistant drafts for a person, and data exposure sets its tier.

The two share a lot. Both send customer personal data to a vendor model behind the same four safeguards, and both score data exposure Critical. Each changed its model once, as a material change. Each recorded an S2 incident that began when a source of truth changed and the system kept quoting the old version. The file runs from intake to six months in production. Read it as a walk written down.

### The change and the incident

[Section 12](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/examples/customer-service-chatbot/governance-case-file.md#12-material-change-revalidation-v120-stage-9) records the material change. Version 1.2.0 moved the base model from `claude-sonnet-4-20250514` to `claude-sonnet-4-5-20250929`, for quality and cost, and retargeted the prompt as v2.0.0. Fourteen dated rows of revalidation follow. They run from the re-tiering check, through a full T1 prompt review, safety and bias re-runs, the fallback revalidated on the new prompt and MRM's addendum, to an out-of-cycle approval by the committee, the CISO and the DPO. It is Step 17's route, taken by choice.

[Sections 14](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/examples/customer-service-chatbot/governance-case-file.md#14-example-incident-s2-customer-facing-hallucination-gen-2026-042-stage-8) and [15](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/examples/customer-service-chatbot/governance-case-file.md#15-post-mortem-gen-2026-042-stage-8) record the incident. Fee schedules changed on 2026-03-01 and never reached the retrieval corpus. The refresh pipeline's token had expired that day, and it failed silently. Two weeks later the 7-day hallucination rate reached 1.1%, over its 1% alert line. About 40 customers had been given wrong fee information. None was charged wrongly. The post-mortem found the gap: monitoring watched output quality, and nothing upstream. The committee accepted the fix, and the framework's monitoring standard gained [RAG pipeline health](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/framework/llm-lifecycle/monitoring.md#rag-pipeline-health).

Set it beside Step 11. The assistant's stale fact sat in its prompt, and a person caught it. The chatbot's sat in its corpus, and a monitor caught it. Both ended in a changed control.

### What a reviewer looks for

Read the file the way the walk reads your own system.

- **Coverage.** Every control the register applies needs a section or a reason. [PM-008](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/framework/governance-operations/control-register.md#stage-8-production-operations), the quarterly fallback switch test for T1 systems on external models, appears in neither list. Section 2 says no switch is recorded, and counts the fallback as degraded because one platform serves both models. Ask for the escalation to AI Risk that PM-008 requires.
- **Order.** Section 9's dated sequence runs from risk assessment to deployment, with validation before the signatures. The red team tested prompt v1.2.0. Its fix was retested on v1.2.3, the version evaluated and shipped.
- **Classification.** Version 1.1.0 added German and updated the corpus. The Head of AI approved it as non-material. The framework's [definition](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/framework/governance-operations/governance-workflow.md#stage-9-ongoing-governance) counts a new user group as a change of scope, and says to treat a doubtful change as material. The next review found German faithfulness at 0.88 against a 0.90 threshold, on 80 cases, too few to gate. Ask whether that release belonged on the material route.
- **Closure.** A fix belongs in the operating record too. Section 10's alert table carries a row for refresh pipeline failure, added after GEN-2026-042.

</DeepDive>

Twenty-two steps in, you have the machinery governance runs on. You have watched one invented system pass through all of it, failures included. What you don't have yet is that trail for a system of your own. So take your highest-risk system to someone who didn't build it. Open the register entry together and ask for the record Step 1 left. Keep going until you reach the permission it runs on today. Write every gap down with an owner, close them, and walk it again.
