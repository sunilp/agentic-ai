Picture a claims team where an AI drafts every settlement letter and a handler approves it. The screen shows the letter and an approve button. The policy wording sits in another system, two logins away. By mid-afternoon one handler has approved 60 letters. She has changed four, all for tone.

On the risk register, the control reads "a human reviews every output". The audit trail agrees. Every letter carries a name and a time.

Now ask when a reviewer last stopped a letter because it was wrong. Nobody can name one. Either the letters are very good or nobody reads them. The register can't tell you which.

So what turns a person in the loop into a control? And how would you know it works?

## Design the human check like a control

A person who reviews an AI's output is a control. It needs an owner, a design and a test.

1. **Pick the pattern your tier allows.** The framework has [four patterns](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/framework/responsible-ai/human-in-loop-patterns.md#hitl-pattern-spectrum), from review of every output to periodic audit. At T1 and T2 its [selection matrix](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/framework/responsible-ai/human-in-loop-patterns.md#pattern-selection-matrix) decides, and where a regulation that applies to you asks for more, the stricter holds. A T1 system that informs customers takes Pattern 1, a person reviewing every output, unless all five Pattern 3 safeguards are in place and evidenced. Record the pattern and the staffing in the oversight document that control [PM-003](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/framework/governance-operations/control-register.md#stage-8-production-operations) requires before traffic starts.
2. **Write down the decision.** One question per review, in the reviewer's words: "Is every fee, date and policy term right for this customer's plan?" Ask people only to check a draft, and they check how it reads.
3. **Put the evidence in view.** The source, the customer's own facts and the passage a claim rests on sit beside the output. The framework's [fatigue controls](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/framework/responsible-ai/human-in-loop-patterns.md#reviewer-fatigue-mitigation) ask for tooling that highlights areas of concern.
4. **Budget the time.** The same controls cap reviews per shift, rotate reviewers and require breaks. Staff for your volume inside those limits. Never set a target for time per review. The framework reads a falling one as a reason to [check thoroughness](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/framework/responsible-ai/human-in-loop-patterns.md#monitoring-hitl-effectiveness).
5. **Make "no" cost nothing.** Pattern 1 gives reviewers full authority to approve, edit or reject. Rejecting should take no more effort than approving. Train every reviewer first, on the framework's [five topics](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/framework/responsible-ai/human-in-loop-patterns.md#reviewer-training) and on automation bias. Control GV-002 keeps an untrained reviewer out of the role.
6. **Test it with planted errors.** Put known-bad outputs in the real queue and count the catches. The framework lists this as a fatigue control and makes it the [default at T1](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/framework/operating-model/ai-literacy.md#system-specific-training-by-tier). Run it without blame, as Step 7 set out. The guide's recommendation: reviewers know drills happen, no name goes on a result, and each planted item sits on a synthetic ticket, so nothing wrong can reach a customer.

**The guide's call: a reviewer you have never seen catch an error is a rubber stamp, not oversight.** A reviewer who reads and one who doesn't make the same click. The override rate can't tell them apart. The framework reads a rate below 2 percent on a T1 system as [possible rubber-stamping](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/framework/operating-model/governance-metrics.md#interpreting-responsible-ai-kpis), a reason to investigate. A low rate also fits a good model. A high one fits a poor model, or reviewers who rewrite every greeting and never check a fee. Only a catch shows the check working. When the system is good, real catches are rare. So plant them.

The step is done when reviewers can, and sometimes do, overrule the system. They can once the decision, the evidence, the time and the authority are in place. They do once you have watched them catch an error, real or planted, in their everyday queue.

**Your call.** Pick the human review your highest-tier system leans on. Write down the last time a reviewer stopped an output because it was wrong, and what was on their screen. Then write down the override rate, if anyone measures it.

## Five things behind a real approval

<ArchitectureDiagram wide src="/assets/diagrams/gov-15-review-control.svg" alt="What a reviewer needs for a review to be a control. Four conditions mean reviewers can overrule the system: the decision, one question written down; the evidence, in view beside the output; the time, a cap per shift; and the authority to say no at no cost. The fifth, the test, planted errors that get caught, shows that they sometimes do. Without each one: the reviewer checks how it reads, an error that looks right goes out, rare errors slip by late in a session, yes becomes the quick answer, and a check and a stamp look the same." caption="Each box is a way the review fails when that condition is missing." />

**The decision.** With no written question, reviewers answer an easier one. A fluent draft passes "does this read well?" whatever its facts.

**The evidence.** If checking means leaving the screen, people check only when something looks wrong. The errors that matter look right.

**The time.** Rare errors slip by, and the framework notes that fatigue wears review down over time. Gate every output with no cap and reviewers stop reading, the failure the [approval gate](/patterns/approval-gate/) pattern warns about.

**The authority.** If a rejection needs a typed reason and an approval needs nothing, yes becomes the quick answer. Send doubtful cases down an [escalation path](/patterns/escalation-path/) with a named owner. A wrong rejection costs minutes. A wrong approval reaches a customer.

**The test.** The first four make a no possible. Only the test shows one. It is the [rubber-stamp verifier](/patterns/rubber-stamp-verifier/) check applied to people: hand the check broken work and see whether it says no.

**Read the override rate as a reason to look.** AI Operations tracks it monthly. The guide recommends splitting it three ways: wording edits, corrected facts, rejections. A wording edit shows someone read the draft. A corrected fact shows someone checked it. The framework's flags, below 2 percent at T1 and above 30 percent anywhere, tell you where to look. They don't tell you what you'll find.

**Run planted rounds like drills.** The guide recommends five habits.

- Copy real failures from the near-miss log and the evaluation results, so each planted error looks like one your system makes.
- Use the real queue and screen. A send on a synthetic ticket goes nowhere and counts as a miss.
- Set the bar first: every planted error caught. For a real error that passes review, the framework's rule is that any occurrence prompts a review of process and training. Apply it to planted ones.
- Record each item's conditions, such as the error type and how far into the session it arrived. Never record the reviewer.
- Afterwards, show everyone the planted drafts and what was wrong.

Twenty planted errors give you no rate worth quoting. They give you misses to learn from. Ask of each what the framework's [blameless review](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/framework/operating-model/incident-forensics.md#blameless-review-principles) asks: why did the system allow this?

Now look at what you wrote. A date, with the source on the reviewer's screen, means your review has been seen to work. An override rate alone is a count of clicks. A blank means you have a signature. Your next move is a planted round.

<RunningExample>
The assistant's pilot runs under Pattern 1. A trained support agent reads each draft and decides what is sent. The numbers here are illustrative.

Step 11's four wrong replies each passed a human review. The override rate was 6 percent, three times the framework's flag, and it raised no question. Most overrides were wording. The rate counted a new greeting the same as a corrected fee.

So the product manager, who owns control PM-003, has the review tested. The AI lead and the two senior agents who rated drafts in Step 10 write 20 known-wrong drafts. Each copies a mistake the assistant has made: a fee from another plan, the old refund window, a waiver the case notes don't show. Each sits on a synthetic ticket addressed to nowhere, spread through the ten agents' queues. The agents learned in training that drills happen. The bar, set beforehand: every error caught, and any miss reviewed.

They catch 17. The 3 misses have two things in common. Each quoted a real fee, the right amount for a different plan. Each arrived late in a long session. The screen showed the email and the draft. The plan terms were a search away.

Three changes follow. The plan terms beside the draft, which the agents asked for in Step 12, ship in the next release. The drill showed what their absence cost. The Head of Customer Support sets a cap on reviews per shift with the agents. And the reject button stops asking for a reason. Approving never did. Training adds the wrong-plan fee to its failure modes. The team sees all 20 drafts. No miss carries a name.

After the release, a second round plants 20 new drafts, wrong-plan fees among them. Every error is caught. Neither round gives a rate. Together they show the fixes working: the kind of error the first round missed, the second caught. The results go into row 14 of the evidence pack, and a round runs each quarter.
</RunningExample>

## Who plants the errors at each size

The five conditions hold at any size. Who plants the errors, and who sees the results, changes.

<Track for="small">
Most of your systems are T3 or T4, where the framework picks the pattern from factors such as volume and the consequence of an error. The review you lean on most is your own. The [acceptable use standard](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/framework/operating-model/acceptable-use.md#ai-coding-assistants) sends AI-generated code through normal code review, and its author answers for it.

The guide's recommendation: once a quarter, someone slips one known-bad output into the real flow, such as a pull request with a bug the tests miss. Rotate who plants. In a team of five everyone knows who missed, so agree first that a miss is a finding about the check.

At T1 or T2, reviewers pass a knowledge check before they act, and at T1 the known-bad checks apply too. Staffed oversight there usually means sharing an operations team, as the framework's [small-team route](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/framework/governance-operations/governance-workflow.md#small-teams) notes.
</Track>

<Track for="enterprise">
The system owner keeps the list of named reviewers and confirms each was trained before acting, as the [literacy standard](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/framework/operating-model/ai-literacy.md#ownership) assigns.

The guide's recommendations: a planted round for each T1 system every quarter, and after any material change to the system or the review screen. Someone outside the reviewers' management line builds it, such as the AI lead with a quality team. Results go to the system owner and the AI Risk Committee by error type and condition, never by person.

Below 85 percent agreement between reviewers, the framework retrains them or clarifies the criteria. Bring planted misses to the same conversation. Report planted catches quarterly, with no target. A target turns a drill into an exam, and people learn to spot exams.
</Track>

<Shortcut quote="Our override rate is well above 2 percent, so the review is working.">
**Why it's tempting.** The framework gives you a flag, and you are on the right side of it. The number arrives every month for free.

**Why it bites.** The flag tells you when to look. Clearing it proves nothing. A rate made of wording edits stays high while nobody checks a fee. Step 11's wrong replies went out under a rate three times the flag.

**The forcing function.** The guide's recommendation: the PM-003 oversight document carries the date and result of the last planted round. Each formal review checks that it is current, and with no round on file, records oversight as untested. Split the rate by kind of edit, so a new greeting never counts as a check.
</Shortcut>

### Automation bias

Linda Skitka, Kathleen Mosier and Mark Burdick [compared error rates](https://doi.org/10.1006/ijhc.1999.0252) in a simulated flight task, with and without a computer that monitored the system and recommended decisions. On the monitoring task, people without the aid beat people with a very but not perfectly reliable one. With the aid, people made errors of omission, missing events it didn't prompt them about. They also made errors of commission, following its recommendations against their training and other valid indicators.

A drafting assistant invites both. The reviewer misses the fee the draft states quietly. Or the draft confirms a waiver, and the reviewer sends it because the draft said so.

Rarity makes it worse. Jeremy Wolfe, Todd Horowitz and Naomi Kenner [had observers](https://doi.org/10.1038/435439a) search for tools among other objects, a lab version of baggage screening. With a tool on half the displays, observers missed 7 percent of them. At 10 percent prevalence, errors rose to 16 percent. At 1 percent they reached 30 percent, almost all misses. The authors called for work on whether real screening behaves the same way. Your queue sits at the rare end, and the better the system, the rarer its errors. Planted errors, kept rare, tell you whether people still catch one.

Explanations don't cure it. Gagan Bansal and colleagues [ran user studies](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.14779) on three datasets, with an AI about as accurate as the people it helped. The AI's help improved the teams. Explanations added nothing to that. They raised the chance that people accepted the AI's recommendation, right or wrong. A rationale beside a draft is one more claim to check.

Friction helps, at a cost people feel. Zana Buçinca, Maja Malaya and Krzysztof Gajos [tested three designs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.09692) with 199 participants, each built to make people engage with the AI's explanation. These cognitive forcing designs reduced overreliance compared with simple explainable-AI designs. Participants rated the ones that reduced it most the least favourably. Judge a review screen by what it catches in a planted round.

### In, on and over the loop

- **In the loop, Pattern 1.** A qualified person reviews every output before use, with authority to approve, edit or reject.
- **On the loop, Pattern 2.** Outputs go out, and people review a statistically valid sample. Some wrong outputs reach users first.
- **Over the loop, Pattern 3.** Automated checks deliver most outputs and escalate the rest. Failure modes nobody anticipated bypass the human.
- **Out of the loop, Pattern 4.** Periodic audit only, for low-consequence work. Never customer-facing.

Further down the list, the person sees fewer outputs and the automation carries more weight. So the test moves too: plant a bad output and see whether the checks route it to a person. Pattern 3 on a T1 customer-facing system keeps people in it: one of its five safeguards is a daily review of at least 1 percent of outputs, minimum 50, by qualified reviewers. That review needs the five conditions too. Agents add gates before actions, which Step 18 takes up.

For high-risk systems, the EU AI Act's [Article 14](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj) requires a design that natural persons can oversee effectively while the system is in use. The system must enable the people assigned, as appropriate and proportionate, "to remain aware of the possible tendency of automatically relying or over-relying on the output", which the Act calls automation bias. It must also enable them "to decide, in any particular situation, not to use the high-risk AI system or to otherwise disregard, override or reverse the output". Article 26(2) has deployers assign oversight to people with "the necessary competence, training and authority, as well as the necessary support".

The support assistant isn't high-risk. Drafting support replies falls in no Annex III area, and the assistant is neither a product nor a safety component of one under Annex I. Article 14 doesn't bind it. The framework's matrix does, and it [maps Article 14](https://github.com/sunilp/ai-governance-framework/blob/v2.4.0/framework/compliance/eu-ai-act-mapping.md#b-provider-of-high-risk-ai-systems) to controls that include PM-003, the one this step builds.

You now have a review you have watched catch errors, and drills to keep watching. A reviewer is the last line, and sometimes a tired one. Some limits can't rest on that. How often a draft may be wrong, what abuse it must withstand and which data it must never reveal belong to the system, set before the run that tests them. Step 16 bounds reliability, safety and privacy.
